What is Karuta?

Karuta is a declarative logic programming language that seamlessly marries the elegance of Prolog with the robustness of the Erlang BEAM virtual machine. Named after the traditional Japanese card game that requires quick pattern recognition, Karuta enables developers to express complex relationships and computations through pattern matching and logical inference.

Unlike imperative languages where you specify how to compute something, Karuta allows you to declare what you want to compute. The language runtime handles the execution strategy, freeing you to focus on the problem domain rather than implementation details.

Built on top of the battle-tested BEAM VM, Karuta inherits decades of production-proven fault tolerance and massive scalability. Whether you're building intelligent systems, knowledge bases, or complex rule engines, Karuta provides the declarative power you need with the reliability you demand.

Relations as Predicates

In Karuta, relations are not tablesthey are predicates in a logical system, each representing facts that can be true or false.

Formal Logical Foundation

Karuta builds on relation theory and simple set theory expressible in first-order predicate logic (SST/FOPL). This formal foundation means your database operations aren't just data transformationsthey're logical inferences with provable correctness guarantees.

Each relation is a predicate that states facts about your domain. Querying becomes logical reasoning: asking what must be true given the facts you've asserted. This is the true relational model as Codd envisioned itdatabases as logical systems, not file managers.

Semantic Consistency

Because relations carry semantic meaning from your conceptual model, Karuta can enforce that your data remains logically consistent. Type checking verifies not just structural correctness, but semantic validity.

Constraints aren't arbitrary rulesthey're formal expressions of your business logic in predicate logic. The system prevents you from asserting contradictions or violating the meaning you've assigned to your data, catching logical errors at compile time rather than runtime.

The Power of Prolog

Declarative Clarity

Prolog's declarative nature allows you to express what you want to achieve rather than how to achieve it. Write specifications, not implementations. The inference engine handles the execution strategy automatically.

Unification & Pattern Matching

Unification is the most powerful pattern matching you'll ever use. It doesn't just match patternsit solves equations, instantiates variables, and discovers relationships bidirectionally. One predicate can work in multiple "modes" automatically.

Backtracking & Search

Built-in backtracking provides automatic search through solution spaces. Write rules for what constitutes a valid solution, and the runtime explores possibilities for you. Perfect for constraint solving, planning, and puzzles.

Natural Recursion

Recursion is the natural way to express iteration in logic programming. Karuta optimizes tail-recursive predicates automatically, giving you the elegance of recursive definitions with the efficiency of loops.

Symbolic Computation

Manipulate symbols and structures directly without serialization overhead. Perfect for compilers, interpreters, theorem provers, and any system that reasons about symbolic information.

Knowledge Representation

Express domain knowledge as facts and rules in a form that closely mirrors human reasoning. Build expert systems, decision support tools, and intelligent agents with unprecedented clarity.

Domino: Relations as Logical Predicates

At the heart of Karuta lies Domino, an implementation of the Semantic-Relational Data Model using Mnesia, bringing formal logic to database design on the BEAM.

From Conceptual to Logical Models

Database design begins with conceptual modelsdescriptions in natural language of object types and their defining properties. But computers can only manipulate abstract symbols algebraically. Domino bridges this gap by formalizing conceptual models as logical models using relation theory and first-order predicate logic.

When you assign the meaning of your conceptual terms (object types, properties) to the abstract symbols of relational theory (domains, attributes, tuples, relations), you create a computable logical model. The symbols acquire interpretation while maintaining their mathematical propertiesenabling both algebraic manipulation and semantic reasoning.

Properties as First-Class Types

Domino makes an ontological commitment to properties: objects are recognized and distinguished by their observable, defining properties. When properties persistently co-occur, we infer an object type and give it a name. This is how human perception actually workswe don't observe "objectness" directly, but infer objects from their properties.

In Domino, relationships between objects are collective properties at a higher level of abstraction. A relationship among primitive objects becomes a property of the compound object they form together. This unification of properties and relationships eliminates the confusion between them that plagues traditional database design.

Semantic Constraints as Logic

Relations in Domino are not passive tablesthey are predicates in a logical system. Semantic constraints enforce that your data conforms to your conceptual model, ensuring logical models faithfully represent business rules. These aren't mere "integrity constraints"they are formal logical expressions that make your database a reasoning system.

Combined with Karuta's logic programming, Domino turns Mnesia into an active knowledge base. Your data doesn't just store factsit reasons about them, validates itself, and responds to queries expressed in pure logic, all while maintaining ACID guarantees and distributed consistency across the BEAM cluster.

Factorial

A classic example showcasing tail recursion and pattern matching

factorial[X, Out] :- int[X], 'factorial loop'[X, 1, Out].

% A nice tail-recursive definition.
'factorial loop'[0, Out, Out] :- int[Out].
'factorial loop'[N, Acc, Out] :-
    multiply[N, Acc, NewAcc],
    neg[1, -1, Minus],
    plus[N, Minus, Prev],
    'factorial loop'[Prev, NewAcc, Out].
